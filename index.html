How Streaming Split
the Billboard Hot 100
in Two

Over 67 years of the pop charts, the typical song's life has collapsed — while a handful of hits linger longer than ever.

For more than two years, one song would not leave. "Lose Control," by Teddy Swims, clung to the Billboard Hot 100 for 112 weeks — long after most listeners had stopped noticing it was still there. It outlasted even the famous marathon hits just behind it, Glass Animals' "Heat Waves" and The Weeknd's "Blinding Lights." And in that same stretch, something stranger was happening at the other end of the chart: nearly half of all the songs that reached the Hot 100 appeared for a single week and then vanished, never to return.

Both things are true at once — and together they describe a chart coming apart at the seams.

For most of the Hot 100's history, hit songs, big and small, lived roughly similar lives. A modest hit and a smash both spent a season on the chart, give or take. That era is over. An analysis of every weekly Hot 100 from 1958 through 2025 shows the typical song's time on the chart collapsing, even as the biggest hits stay near the top longer than ever. The chart didn't get faster or slower. It split in two.

The break is clearest when you track two lines over time: the typical song, and the top hits. [CHART 1: the line chart] For half a century, they rose and fell together. The typical song's run peaked at 20 weeks in 2001 and 2002 — about five months on the chart. Then it routinely fell of into oblivion. By the 2020s, the typical Hot 100 song lasted just two to three weeks. The biggest hits barely budged: the longest-lasting tier of songs has held between 21 and 30 weeks since the 1990s. In 1995, a top hit outlasted a typical song by less than two to one. By 2021, the gap was nearly eleven to one.

Plot all 32,000-plus songs individually and the divide becomes a landscape. [CHART 2: the scatter] The early decades form a tidy band. After 2010, the picture bifurcates: a dense, swelling cloud of songs clinging to the chart for a week or two, and a thin scatter of outliers climbing higher and higher above it. One-week songs, essentially nonexistent in the late 1970s and '80s, made up 46 percent of the chart's traffic in 2020. The share of songs lasting 20 weeks or more, which peaked around 54 percent in 2001, has fallen to roughly 17 percent. And the sheer number of songs cycling through the chart each year has nearly doubled.

What happened is, in a word, streaming — but it arrived in two waves. The first crack came from downloads. When iTunes made buying a single as easy as a click in the mid-2000s, listeners began churning through songs faster, and one-week entries started ticking up. The larger rupture came when Billboard rebuilt the Hot 100 around streaming: on-demand audio was folded into the formula in March 2012, and YouTube views followed in February 2013. By around 2016, streaming was how most people listened.

Streaming rewired the chart in two opposite directions at once. On one side, it created the "album bomb": when a major artist drops a record now, all of its tracks flood the Hot 100 on a single week's streaming surge, then evaporate — manufacturing one-week entries by the dozen. On the other, streaming lets a genuine phenomenon accumulate weeks indefinitely, as listeners return to the same song month after month. The result is a chart with no middle. Songs either explode or evaporate.

One caveat is worth stating plainly: not all of the giants' new staying power comes from listeners. Billboard has repeatedly changed its rules for when older songs are retired from the chart, so the chart itself now permits songs to linger longer than it once did. (This analysis also sets aside the current year, because those songs are still charting — their runs aren't finished.)

Still, the shape of the change is unmistakable. The slow-building hit — the song that climbs for months as a country discovers it together — is becoming a relic. We used to share a handful of songs across a whole season. Now we share a few of them seemingly forever, and the rest for barely a weekend.
